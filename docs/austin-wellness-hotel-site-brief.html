The headline

Your binding constraint is not price, availability or zoning. It is impervious cover.

West Austin — Barton Hills, Barton Creek, Lost Creek, much of the 360 corridor — sits inside the Barton Springs Zone, governed by the Save Our Springs ordinance. It caps impervious cover at:

Where Cap
Edwards Aquifer recharge zone (incl. Barton Hills) 15%
Barton Creek watershed 20%
BSZ contributing zone 25%
Water Supply zones (Lake Austin watershed, north of the river) 20–40%

Impervious cover means everything: building footprint, parking, drives, service areas, pool decks, hard terraces.

What that does to your programme

Working the numbers for a 50-key wellness hotel — ~40,000–55,000 ft² GFA (rooms, spa, F&B, back of house), plus 120–170 parking spaces, because a membership spa parks far more cars than 50 rooms do:

Regime Land needed, surface parking With structured parking
BSZ recharge — 15% 10.6 – 16.9 acres 5.6 – 10.3 acres
Barton Creek — 20% 7.9 – 12.7 acres 4.2 – 7.7 acres
BSZ contributing — 25% 6.3 – 10.1 acres 3.3 – 6.2 acres
Water Supply — 40% 4.0 – 6.3 acres 2.1 – 3.9 acres
Grandfathered existing commercial (~58%) 2.7 – 4.4 acres 1.4 – 2.7 acres

Three conclusions fall straight out of this table.

1. In Barton Hills, a 5-acre site is not a small version of this project. It is a different, impossible project. At 15% cover, five acres gives you 32,670 ft² of total impervious area — less than the parking alone. Inside the BSZ recharge zone you need to be at the very top of your stated range, 12–17 acres, unless you build structured parking.

2. Structured or tucked-under parking is the single highest-leverage design decision available to you. It roughly halves land requirement. On a constrained West Austin site the cost per stall is very likely cheaper than the incremental acreage — that comparison should be run before you shortlist.

3. An existing building beats raw land, decisively. See below.

The strategic insight: buy the impervious cover, not the dirt

Austin City Code § 25-8-26 permits a redevelopment exception in the Barton Springs Zone for existing commercial, retail and office uses. The condition: redevelopment may not increase the site’s existing impervious cover — but it may keep it.

Older grandfathered commercial and office sites in the BSZ, built before SOS and without water-quality controls, average around 58% impervious cover.

That is the whole game. On a 5-acre site:

  • Raw land, BSZ recharge: 15% → 32,670 ft² of impervious cover.
  • Existing office building, grandfathered: ~58% → ~126,000 ft².

Nearly four times the buildable envelope, on the same five acres. That cover cannot be recreated, bought, or permitted onto vacant land at any price. It exists only where a building already stands.

So your line — “office building or industrial space could work as well” — should be promoted from an alternative to the primary strategy anywhere inside the Barton Springs Zone. A tired 1980s office block on Loop 360 or Bee Cave Road with high existing cover is a far better wellness-hotel site than a prettier greenfield tract next door.

Diligence follow-ons if you take this path:

  • Confirm the existing impervious cover by survey, not by listing claim. It is the asset you are buying.
  • Confirm the redevelopment exception applies to a change of use to hotel, not merely to like-for-like office. Get this in writing from Watershed Protection and Development Services early — it is the assumption the whole strategy rests on.
  • Vested rights attach to a project, not a property (LDC 25-1 Art. 12; Tex. Loc. Gov’t Code ch. 245). Do not assume they transfer on sale.

Geography: where this can and cannot happen

Rule out first — West Lake Hills and Rollingwood city limits

Both are separate incorporated cities, not Austin. Each is almost entirely residential with a thin commercial strip on Bee Cave Road, governed by their own codes (West Lake Hills: B-1/B-2/B-3; Rollingwood: C-1/C-2), and both have small, development-averse councils. Rollingwood bans short-term rentals outright with fines to $2,000/day; West Lake Hills permits them only by special use permit — a fair read of institutional posture toward transient lodging.

A 50-key hotel in either almost certainly requires a zoning change from a council with every incentive to refuse. Treat both as out of scope unless you are prepared to underwrite a multi-year entitlement fight.

“Westlake” colloquially means much more than the two city limits — the Eanes ISD catchment, the 360 and Bee Cave Road corridors, Lost Creek, Barton Creek. Most of that is City of Austin or unincorporated Travis County, and those are workable. Be precise about which you mean when briefing brokers.

Target zones, ranked

1 — Loop 360 / Bee Cave Road commercial corridor (City of Austin). Best fit for the grandfathered-cover play. Existing office inventory, 10–20 min downtown, genuine Westlake address and adjacency, real signal value for $500 ADR. Watershed varies parcel by parcel — check each.

2 — Lake Austin watershed / City Park Road / north of the river. Water Supply zone: 20–40% cover, versus 15% in the BSZ. That difference is worth more than almost any price concession. Lake Austin Spa Resort sits here and is your closest operating proof of concept. Water frontage or canyon views support rate. 15–25 min downtown.

3 — Unincorporated Travis County, west. No city zoning; private deed restrictions govern instead. Larger tracts, more acreage per dollar. But septic/OSSF, well or WCID water, and Austin ETJ status all become live questions — and ETJ status is currently being litigated (see the main guide, §19).

4 — Bee Cave / Lakeway (own municipalities). More development-friendly than Westlake, ~25–30 min downtown — at or slightly past your limit. Worth including in alerts as a fallback.

Deprioritise: Barton Hills proper. The address is excellent and the constraint is the worst in the metro — 15% cover, and the neighbourhood is organised and vocal on Barton Springs matters. Only pursue with a grandfathered building or 12+ acres.

The market read

Austin’s transient hotel market is soft: metro ADR ~$163, RevPAR ~$101, ~1,600 new rooms landing in 2026 concentrated downtown, and the Convention Center closed until 2029. On the surface that looks hostile.

It mostly is not, for this project, for two reasons.

You are not in that comp set. $500+ ADR wellness in Austin means Miraval Austin (117 keys, 220 acres, all-inclusive), Commodore Perry Estate (~$512/night observed), Lake Austin Spa Resort, Hotel Saint Cecilia. That is a thin, supply- constrained segment with no meaningful new entrants — quite unlike the downtown select-service pipeline driving the metro averages down.

Membership changes the risk shape. Recurring local membership revenue is structurally insulated from transient RevPAR cycles, and it is the strongest argument for a West Austin location specifically: Westlake/Eanes has the household income density to support a local membership base that does not depend on visitors at all. The hotel monetises the real estate; the membership de-risks it.

One genuine caution. Downtown softness and the convention-centre gap will compress transient demand through 2029. Underwrite the membership as the base case and room revenue as the upside, not the reverse.

On timing: soft market, elevated inventory and high rates make this a good window to acquire. That is the same logic as the residential searches, and it argues for moving on site control while sellers are negotiable.

Screening checklist

Run in this order. Anything failing 1–4 is dead regardless of how good it looks.

1. Watershed and impervious cover. Which watershed, which zone, what cap, and what cover exists today. Austin’s watershed regulation map plus a survey. This is the first question, not the fifth.

2. Wastewater. A 50-key hotel with a spa is water- and effluent-intensive. City sewer with written capacity, or a WCID/MUD with confirmed capacity. On septic, a commercial OSSF for this load in the Hill Country is a serious constraint and may be fatal.

3. Hotel use. Permitted by right, or a credible entitlement path. Inside Austin, hotel-motel is a listed commercial use (CS-1 among others). Outside, check the municipality. Deed restrictions can bar lodging independently of zoning.

4. Critical Water Quality Zone / floodplain. Creek setbacks can sterilise the developable portion of an otherwise excellent parcel — and a creek is exactly what makes a wellness site attractive. Check both the FEMA maps and Austin’s stricter Atlas 14 mapping.

5. Outdoor activation. Your differentiator. Confirm the usable outdoor area sits outside CWQZ and floodplain, that heritage trees don’t cross the building envelope (Austin protects large-diameter trees; they will reshape a site plan), and that grade is workable — West Austin is steep, and slope both drives cost and triggers additional restrictions.

6. Access and traffic. A Traffic Impact Analysis is likely. Loop 360 and Bee Cave Road access points are contentious and TxDOT may be involved.

7. Rate support. Views, water, canyon, or genuine seclusion. $500 ADR is bought with landscape and privacy, not with finishes.

8. Carrying cost. ~2% of assessed value annually, and commercial appraisal rises with entitlement. Land held through a 2–4 year entitlement and build is expensive to carry — model it explicitly.

What I would do first

  1. Pull Austin’s watershed regulation map and mark the BSZ boundary against your target zones. One afternoon; it will eliminate half your search area and change where you look.
  2. Get § 25-8-26 confirmed in writing — specifically whether the redevelopment exception survives a change of use to hotel. The grandfathered-cover strategy stands or falls on this single answer, and it is free to ask.
  3. Run the structured-parking trade — cost per stall against cost per acre in your target zones. It sets your true minimum site size.
  4. Brief two hotel brokers and one West Austin land broker with the constraint set, not just the wish list.
  5. Screen existing office and industrial stock on Loop 360 and Bee Cave Road by existing impervious cover, before looking at price.
