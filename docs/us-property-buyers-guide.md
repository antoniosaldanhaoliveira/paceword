# The Property Buyer's Guide to the United States

### With dedicated parts on Texas and on Austin

*Written for a buyer who already knows how to buy property in Portugal.*
*Research current as of July 2026. Every rate, threshold and deadline in this
document changes — verify at purchase time. Nothing here is legal or tax advice.*

---

## Part 0 — Read this first

If you have run a Portuguese search, you have a method: pick a narrow zone,
watch it daily, learn what a square metre costs, and use documents to verify
what agents tell you. That method transfers completely. The facts underneath it
do not.

Five differences reshape everything:

| | Portugal | United States |
|---|---|---|
| **Listing data** | Fragmented across portals, agencies duplicate | Centralised in regional **MLS**, syndicated everywhere |
| **Sold prices** | Public | **Depends on the state.** In 12 or so non-disclosure states, including **Texas**, they are not public at all |
| **Acquisition cost** | High — IMT + stamp duty, up to ~8% | Low — many states, Texas included, have **no transfer tax** |
| **Holding cost** | Low — IMI ~0.3–0.45%/yr | **High — 1–2.5%/yr** of assessed value in much of the country |
| **The hard question** | *Is this legally what it claims to be?* | *What is it really worth, and what will it cost me to hold?* |

In Portugal you spend your diligence budget on the **caderneta predial**, the
**certidão permanente** and the **PDM** — proving the thing exists as described.
In the US, recording is reliable and title insurance covers you. You spend your
diligence budget instead on **value discovery** and **carrying cost**.

There is one more difference that has no Portuguese equivalent at all, and it
changed in 2024: **how buyer's agents get paid.** See §7.

# PART ONE — THE UNITED STATES

## 1. There is no "US property market"

The most common mistake, and one that survives even long familiarity with the
country, is treating the US as one market. It is roughly 3,000 counties, each
with its own tax rate, recording office, disclosure rules and appraisal
authority; 50 states with different contract law; and 500+ regional MLSs.

Practical consequences:

- **Property tax varies by a factor of ten.** Roughly 0.3% of value per year in
  Hawaii, over 2% in parts of Texas, New Jersey and Illinois. On a $500,000
  property that is the difference between $1,500 and $11,000 *per year*.
- **Disclosure of sold prices varies.** See §4 — this determines whether you can
  do your own comparables research at all.
- **What "buildable" means varies.** Some jurisdictions have strict zoning;
  Texas counties have almost none, and private deed restrictions do the work
  instead.
- **Natural hazard exposure varies enormously**, and increasingly determines
  whether a property is insurable — which determines whether it is financeable,
  which determines what it is worth.

So: **choose the metro first, then apply the method.** Do not search "USA."

## 2. Step one — define the strategy

Same discipline as Portugal, different variables. Write these down before you
look at a single listing.

1. **Purpose.** Primary residence · second home · rental income · land banking ·
   build-to-own. In the US this is not a preference, it is a tax status. The
   homestead exemption, the mortgage products available to you, and the capital
   gains treatment all key off it.
2. **Residency and tax status.** See §9. As a US person your financing options
   are the full menu and the homestead exemption is available to you — but only
   on a primary residence, which makes this a decision, not a formality.
3. **Metro, then submarket, then zone.** Keep the final search zone tight. The
   Portuguese 15–20 km rule holds; in US terms, **one metro and two or three
   named submarkets**, not "Central Texas."
4. **Property type.** Existing single-family home · condo/townhome · vacant lot ·
   acreage/ranch · multi-family (2–4 units). The diligence paths diverge sharply
   — land is a completely different investigation from a house.
5. **Budget — with holding cost included.** This is the discipline the
   Portuguese market lets you skip and the US market punishes. Compute
   **purchase price + 10 years of property tax + insurance**. A cheaper property
   in a high-tax district frequently loses to a dearer one.
6. **Financing route,** decided before you search. Cash, conventional, FHA/VA,
   jumbo, or — for land — a local bank, Farm Credit lender or seller financing.
   Each changes the price band you can act on, and sellers treat pre-approved
   buyers very differently. Note that land and houses are financed on completely
   different terms (§9).

## 3. Step two — where to search: the portal landscape

This is the research core of this guide. US listing infrastructure has a
specific shape: **almost everything flows from the MLS**, and the consumer
portals are windows onto it with different refresh rates, different extra data,
and different agendas.

### 3.1 How the plumbing works

A listing agent enters a property into the **regional MLS**. That record
syndicates outward — to Realtor.com (operated under NAR licence), to Zillow, to
Redfin, to brokerage sites via **IDX** feeds. This is why the same property
appears everywhere with the same photos.

What differs between portals is **latency, completeness, and what they add**:

- Realtor.com refreshes from MLS roughly every 15 minutes and is generally the
  most accurate on **status** (pending, contingent, sold).
- Redfin refreshes very fast (~5 minutes) and is a brokerage, so it carries real
  MLS depth — including **sold prices**.
- Zillow has the largest audience and inventory (~210M monthly visits) but a
  slower refresh (~30 minutes), so you will see "active" listings already under
  contract.

**Off-MLS inventory exists** and is where less-competitive deals live: FSBO,
auction, county tax sales, land specialists, pocket listings, and rural
properties whose sellers never engaged an agent.

### 3.2 Tier 1 — national MLS-fed portals

Use all four. They are not redundant; they disagree, and the disagreements are
informative.

| Portal | Strength | Weakness | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Zillow** | Largest inventory and audience; best saved-search alerts; Zestimate | Slowest status refresh; Zestimate median error ~7–8% off-market | Primary alert channel; breadth |
| **Realtor.com** | Direct MLS feed, ~15-min refresh; most accurate status | Weaker analytics | **Status truth** — is it actually still available |
| **Redfin** | ~5-min refresh; **shows sold prices**; strong data; low listing fees | Only where Redfin operates as a brokerage | **Comparables** — critical in non-disclosure states |
| **Homes.com** | "Your listing, your lead" model, agent-friendly; growing | Newer, less analytic depth | Cross-check; sometimes surfaces listings first |

**Trulia** is Zillow-owned and shares its inventory — neighbourhood-feel data,
not a separate source. **Movoto**, **Compass**, **Coldwell Banker**, **eXp** and
other brokerage sites run on the same IDX feeds; useful for a specific agent's
pocket listings, not as a separate search.

**On automated valuations (Zestimate, Redfin Estimate):** median error is around
7–8% for off-market homes. On a $500k property that is ±$40,000. Treat them as a
sanity check, never as a valuation. They are worst exactly where you need them
most — unusual properties and vacant land.

### 3.3 Tier 2 — land and rural specialists

Vacant land is badly served by the residential portals. Much rural inventory
never reaches Zillow at all. If you are buying land, these are not optional.

| Site | Coverage | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Land.com network** — LandWatch, Lands of America, Land and Farm | The deepest rural inventory in the US; millions of acres | Three brands, one company, overlapping listings. LandWatch syndicates Lands of America. Start here for acreage |
| **LandSearch** | 10M+ acres; clean map interface, good filters | Best UI of the land sites |
| **AcreValue** | Parcel maps, ownership, **soil data**, valuation | Free tier is genuinely useful. Built for agricultural land |
| **LandFlip / Landmodo / Land Century** | Smaller inventory, owner-financed deals | Owner financing is common in raw land; also where the worst listings are |
| **Whitetail Properties, Mossy Oak, National Land Realty** | Brokerage networks specialising in recreational/hunting/timber land | Regional strength; agents who actually know the parcels |

Land listings have **much worse data hygiene** than house listings — missing
acreage, wrong boundaries, no utility information, stale prices. Verify every
figure against the county appraisal district and the parcel map.

### 3.4 Tier 3 — distressed, auction and government inventory

Lower competition, higher risk, and usually **no inspection contingency**. Not a
beginner's channel, but worth knowing.

| Source | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Auction.com** | Largest online foreclosure platform; bank-owned and courthouse-step auctions. Buyer premium typically 3–5% on top of the bid |
| **Xome** | Foreclosure, REO, short sale, plus MLS listings |
| **Hubzu** (Altisource) | REO, short sale, deed-in-lieu, CWCOT auctions |
| **HUD Home Store** (hudhomestore.gov) | HUD-owned homes; owner-occupant priority windows |
| **Fannie Mae HomePath** / **Freddie Mac HomeSteps** | Government-sponsored-enterprise REO |
| **County sheriff sales / tax sales** | Cheapest and riskiest. County-by-county rules. In Texas, tax sales are first Tuesday of the month at the courthouse |

Rules for this tier: assume you cannot inspect the interior; assume you buy
subject to existing liens unless you have verified otherwise; **always** run a
title search first; and budget for the property being in far worse condition
than the photographs.

### 3.5 Tier 4 — data and parcel tools (the real edge)

This is where a disciplined buyer separates from a browser. All of these are
free or nearly free.

| Tool | Gives you |
|---|---|
| **County appraisal district / assessor** | The authoritative parcel record: legal description, acreage, improvements, assessed value, exemptions, and **every taxing entity on the parcel**. In Texas: TCAD (Travis), HaysCAD, WilCAD, BastropCAD |
| **County clerk records** | Recorded deeds, plats, easements, restrictive covenants, liens |
| **County GIS map** | Parcel boundaries, zoning overlays, floodplain layers |
| **Regrid / Landgrid** | Nationwide parcel data from county sources; export to CSV/KML |
| **LandGlide** (~$10/mo) | **Property lines on your phone by GPS**, 99% parcel coverage — walk the boundary in real time. The single most useful field tool for land |
| **FEMA Flood Map Service Center** | Official federal flood zone. Note: local maps are often stricter |
| **USGS / NRCS Web Soil Survey** | Soil type, slope, septic suitability |
| **NOAA / state climate offices** | Rainfall, drought, hazard history |
| **Census / BLS / local economic development** | Population, employment, migration — the demand side |

### 3.6 Tier 5 — off-market and private sellers

- **FSBO sites** — ForSaleByOwner.com, FSBO.com. Thin, but no listing agent.
- **Craigslist** and **Facebook Marketplace** — genuinely productive for rural
  land and mobile/manufactured homes. Zero data discipline. This is the OLX
  equivalent.
- **Direct approach.** Identify a parcel you want via GIS, find the owner in the
  appraisal district record, write to them. Entirely legal, and how a large
  share of rural land actually trades.
- **Local land brokers.** In rural markets the inventory in a broker's head
  exceeds what is online.

### 3.7 The search discipline

1. Build one saved search **per portal per submarket**, with email alerts on.
2. Name each alert after the search so incoming mail self-sorts.
3. Review daily. Ten minutes. The value is in repetition, exactly as in
   Portugal — you are training a price instinct, and it takes roughly 60–80
   observed listings in one zone before you have one.
4. Record every listing you seriously consider, including the ones you reject.
   Rejections are half the data.
5. **Do not crawl the portals.** Zillow, Redfin and Realtor.com all forbid
   automated harvesting and block quickly. The email alerts are the intended and
   reliable intake channel.

## 4. Step three — study the market (and the disclosure trap)

**In roughly 12 states, sale prices are not public record.** Texas is one. So
are Idaho, Kansas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Missouri, Montana, New Mexico, North
Dakota, Utah and Wyoming (lists vary at the margins; some are partial).

In a **disclosure state**, you can pull sold prices from public records and
build your own comparables — much like Portugal.

In a **non-disclosure state**, you cannot. The county clerk does not record the
price and the appraisal district publishes only its own *assessed* value, which
is an estimate, not a transaction. Only licensed agents can pull sold prices,
from the MLS.

This is the most important structural fact for a self-directed buyer in Texas,
and it has three consequences:

1. **Your own medians are asking-price medians.** Asking prices sit above
   closing prices — in a soft market, well above. Never read "15% below the
   median" as "15% below market value."
2. **Redfin becomes disproportionately valuable**, because as a brokerage it
   displays sold prices from its MLS feed. It is the best free comparables
   source you have.
3. **You need one MLS-side relationship.** An agent who will send you sold comps
   is the highest-value input in the entire search. Failing that, a paid
   appraisal or broker price opinion.

Where price data is thin, **time is the honest signal.** Days on market, number
of price cuts, and the size of those cuts tell you what the list price will not.
A property 150 days in with two reductions is telling you its true number.

### What to measure

- **$/ft²** for built property; **$/acre** for rural land; **$/ft²** for urban
  infill lots. Never mix acres and square feet in one median — the numbers
  differ by a factor of 43,560 and the pooled result is meaningless.
- **Days on market**, and the *distribution*, not just the median.
- **Sold-to-list ratio**, if you can get it. Above 100% is a hot market; below
  97% means sellers are negotiating.
- **Months of inventory.** Under 4 favours sellers; 4–6 is balanced; over 6
  favours buyers. This single number should set your negotiating posture.

## 5. Step four — visiting

Everything in the Portuguese guide applies. Additions specific to the US:

- **Walk the boundary with LandGlide or equivalent.** Fences are frequently not
  on property lines. This is a five-minute check that has saved buyers years of
  litigation.
- **Visit in the rain**, or at least look hard at where water goes. Drainage
  problems are invisible on a dry day and no map shows them at parcel scale.
- **Check the neighbours' land use.** Rural US zoning is often nonexistent — the
  quiet tract next door can legally become a feedlot, a quarry or a shooting
  range.
- **Confirm cell signal and internet.** Fibre/cable availability, or Starlink
  viability, is a genuine value factor in rural America.
- **Drive the commute at the actual hour** you would drive it.
- Ask the seller directly: **what does it cost you per year to own this?** Their
  tax bill and insurance premium are the two numbers that matter most.

## 6. Step five — track everything

Same principle as Portugal: a listing you did not record teaches you nothing.
Track at minimum — source, URL, ID, type, county, price, first price, area with
its **unit**, $/unit, days on market, status, flags, and every price change with
its date.

The repository this guide sits in automates exactly this; see
`.claude/skills/pt-property-tracker/`.

## 7. The agent question — and what changed in 2024

This has no Portuguese equivalent and foreign buyers consistently get it wrong.

Historically, the seller paid both agents, so a buyer's agent appeared free. The
**NAR settlement, effective 17 August 2024**, ended that arrangement:

- **Buyer-agent compensation can no longer be advertised on the MLS.**
- **You must sign a written buyer-broker agreement before touring a home**, and
  it must state your agent's compensation specifically and objectively — not
  open-ended.
- Sellers may still contribute to your agent's fee, but it is now negotiated
  privately, deal by deal.

What this means practically:

1. **Negotiate your buyer-agent fee**, and negotiate it before you sign. It is
   now an explicit line item, typically 2–3% but genuinely variable.
2. **Ask the seller to cover it** as a term of your offer. Very common; in a
   buyer's market, often granted.
3. **Read the agreement's term and exclusivity.** A 12-month exclusive across a
   whole metro is a bad deal. Ask for a shorter term or a narrower area.
4. **The listing agent is not your agent.** "Dual agency" is legal in some
   states and prohibited in others. A buyer's agent who owes you fiduciary duty
   is worth paying for, particularly in a non-disclosure state where they are
   also your comparables source.

## 8. The transaction

Broad shape; state variations are real (see Part Two for Texas).

1. **Pre-approval** from a lender, or proof of funds if cash. Sellers routinely
   ignore offers without it.
2. **Offer**, on a state-promulgated or attorney-drafted contract, with
   contingencies: inspection, financing, appraisal, title, survey.
3. **Escrow opens.** Earnest money — typically 1–3% — goes to a neutral third
   party, a **title company** or **escrow agent** in most states, a **closing
   attorney** in others.
4. **Due diligence window.** Inspection, survey, title commitment review,
   insurance quote. This is short. Order everything on day one.
5. **Appraisal**, if financed. A low appraisal renegotiates or kills the deal.
6. **Title commitment and insurance.** Read Schedule B — the exceptions — where
   easements, restrictions and mineral reservations appear.
7. **Final walkthrough**, then **closing**: sign, fund, record.

**Closing costs**, buyer side, typically 2–5% of price: lender fees, appraisal,
title insurance, escrow, recording, prepaid tax and insurance escrows. Plus a
transfer tax in many states — **but not in Texas**.

## 9. Money and financing

As a **US person**, this is the part of the transaction that looks most like
what you would expect and least like the Portuguese process. Your leverage is
better than a foreign buyer's, and materially better than a cash-only buyer's
in a market with six months of inventory.

### Financing a house

| Product | Down payment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Conventional** | 3–20% | Under 20% triggers PMI until you reach ~20% equity. The default path |
| **FHA** | 3.5% | Easier credit thresholds; mortgage insurance for the life of most loans |
| **VA** | 0% | If eligible, the best terms available in the US. No PMI |
| **Jumbo** | 10–20% | Above the conforming limit — relevant inside Austin city limits, where the median is ~$595,000 |

Get **pre-approved, not pre-qualified**, before you search. Pre-qualification is
an opinion; pre-approval is underwritten and sellers treat the two very
differently. In Austin's current market a clean pre-approval plus flexibility on
closing date is worth real money off the price.

### Financing land — the part that surprises people

**Vacant land is much harder to finance than a house**, and this is the single
biggest practical difference between your two searches.

- Most residential lenders will not touch raw land at all.
- Land loans that exist typically want **20–50% down**, run shorter terms
  (often 10–20 years, sometimes with a balloon), and carry higher rates.
- **Local and regional banks, Farm Credit lenders, and credit unions** are the
  realistic sources — not the national mortgage brands.
- **Seller financing is common** in rural land and often the best terms
  available. Always ask whether the seller will carry paper.
- A **construction-to-permanent loan** can wrap the land and the build into one
  facility if you intend to build reasonably soon. Usually better than buying
  the land on a land loan and refinancing later.

Budget accordingly: a $400,000 tract may need $120,000–200,000 in cash, where a
$400,000 house needs $12,000–80,000.

### Tax position

- **Homestead exemption applies to you** on a primary residence — file for it,
  it is not automatic, and it brings the 10% annual appraisal cap with it
  (§16). If you buy both a house and land, only the residence gets it.
- **No FIRPTA withholding** on your eventual sale, and no ITIN complications.
- **Texas SB 17 does not restrict you** (§13).
- **Mortgage interest and property tax** may be deductible depending on your
  overall position and whether you itemise — worth a conversation with a CPA
  before you choose between cash and financing.
- **Capital gains**: the primary-residence exclusion (currently $250k single /
  $500k married, with a two-of-five-years occupancy test) is one of the better
  deals in the US tax code, and it does not apply to land you never live on.
- **LLC ownership** for the land, if you want liability separation, is worth
  discussing — but note it can **forfeit the homestead exemption** and
  complicate financing. Do not put a primary residence in an LLC casually.

### If you later buy as a non-US entity or with foreign partners

Not your situation, but worth knowing it exists: foreign nationals can buy US
property with no visa or residency, using foreign-national full-doc, DSCR or
ITIN mortgage products (typically 15–30% down). They face **FIRPTA withholding
on sale**, a very small US estate tax exemption on US-situs assets, and — in
Texas — the SB 17 restrictions if connected to a designated country. If any
future purchase involves foreign partners or entities, take advice before
structuring it.

## 10. Costs — the shape is inverted

| | Portugal | Typical US |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase taxes | IMT up to ~7.5% + 0.8% stamp | 0–2% transfer tax; **none in Texas** |
| Closing costs | Notary, registration, lawyer ~2–3% | 2–5% |
| **Annual property tax** | ~0.3–0.45% | **1–2.5%** |
| Insurance | Modest | Substantial, and rising fast in hazard-exposed markets |
| Agent fee (buyer) | Usually none | **2–3%, now explicitly negotiated** |

**Insurance deserves its own line.** In a growing number of US markets — Florida,
California, Louisiana, parts of Texas — insurance availability, not price, is
becoming the binding constraint. An uninsurable property is unfinanceable, and
an unfinanceable property has a fraction of the buyer pool at resale. Get a
**real, bindable quote** during your diligence window, never an estimate.

# PART TWO — TEXAS

## 11. Why Texas, and what it costs

The pitch is real: no state income tax, strong job growth, no transfer tax, and
a business-friendly regulatory posture. The bill arrives annually.

**Texas funds itself through property tax.** Effective rates run roughly
1.5–2.5% of assessed value depending on the taxing entities on your parcel. On a
$500,000 property that is $7,500–12,500 *every year*, and the appraisal district
adjusts the taxable value toward market annually.

For a buyer from Portugal, invert your mental model: **buying is cheap, owning
is expensive.** Budget accordingly, and treat the tax rate as a search filter,
not a closing detail.

## 12. The non-disclosure problem, concretely

Texas does not record sale prices. Not with the county clerk, not with the
appraisal district. See §4 for why this matters; in Texas specifically:

- **TCAD/HaysCAD/WilCAD give you facts, not prices** — legal description,
  acreage, improvement square footage, exemptions, and the full list of taxing
  entities. Invaluable, but the "value" shown is the district's estimate.
- **Redfin is your best free comparables source**, because it shows sold prices
  from its MLS feed.
- **Get an agent who will send you sold comps.** In Texas this is not a
  convenience, it is the missing half of your data.

A perverse upside: because the appraisal district is estimating your value
without knowing what you paid, **its estimate is frequently wrong** — which is
why protesting is routine and often successful (§16).

## 13. SB 17 — foreign ownership restrictions

**Effective 1 September 2025**, Texas restricts acquisition of real property by
persons and entities tied to **designated countries** — currently **China,
Russia, Iran and North Korea**, plus any country designated by the Governor or
identified by the Director of National Intelligence as a national security risk.

Key points:

- It covers commercial, agricultural **and residential** property, plus water,
  groundwater and mineral rights, and leases of one year or longer.
- It reaches **indirect and partial** ownership through entities.
- There is an **exception for a primary residence** by an individual lawfully
  present in the US.
- Enforcement lies with the Attorney General. A purchase in violation is not
  automatically void, but a **leasehold** in violation is void and unenforceable.
- Sellers have no duty to investigate — so **the compliance burden is yours**.
- It has been challenged in federal court; at least one challenge was dismissed.
  The list of designated countries can grow.

**As a US person, this does not restrict you.** You will still likely sign a
title-company affidavit at closing confirming it. It matters in two situations:
if you ever take on foreign partners or investors in an entity that holds Texas
property, and if you sell — a restricted buyer pool is a smaller buyer pool.
The list of designated countries can grow.

## 14. The Texas transaction — TREC, the option period, title

**Contracts are promulgated by TREC** (Texas Real Estate Commission). Residential
resales use the *One to Four Family Residential Contract (Resale)*. Agents fill
in blanks; they do not draft terms. The form is public — read it before you
make an offer.

**The option period is the buyer's great advantage in Texas.**

- You pay an **option fee** — commonly $100–$500, paid to the seller — for an
  **unrestricted right to terminate** for any reason.
- Typical length **7–10 days**, negotiable.
- Terminate within it and you forfeit only the option fee; **earnest money is
  returned**.
- The option fee is normally credited toward the purchase at closing.
- Earnest money (typically 1–2%) and the option fee must be delivered within
  **3 days of the effective date**. These deadlines are strict.

**Rules:** never waive the option period to win a bidding war unless every
diligence item is already resolved — it is your only unconditional exit. And
order the inspection and survey review on **day one**, not day five.

**Texas is a title company state.** A licensed title company or escrow officer
closes the transaction; no attorney is required. They prepare documents, hold
escrow, and record the deed. Title insurance rates in Texas are **promulgated by
the state**, so the premium is the same everywhere — shop on service, not price.

**Documents to demand:** title commitment (read Schedule B exceptions and
Schedule C requirements), **survey plus T-47 affidavit** from the seller — if
they will not sign one, buy a new survey — Seller's Disclosure Notice, tax
certificate, restrictive covenants, HOA resale certificate, and any statutory
notices (**MUD**, PID, annexation, water district).

**No transfer tax.** Budget instead for the owner's title policy, survey, lender
fees and escrows.

## 15. Land in Texas — water, septic, access

For rural land the binding constraints are physical, not regulatory.

**Water.** Texas groundwater is governed by the **rule of capture**, modified by
local groundwater conservation districts. Ask: is there a well, and what does its
log, depth, static level and current flow test say? Or is there a written
**service availability letter** from a water utility for *this parcel*?
Neighbouring wells predict nothing.

**Wastewater.** Outside sewer service you need an **OSSF** (septic) permit from
the county. Get a licensed site evaluation with soil borings before you commit.
Shallow limestone and slope defeat conventional drainfields across the Hill
Country, pushing you to an aerobic system with ongoing maintenance obligations.

**Access.** Confirm the tract touches a public road or has a **recorded,
permanent easement** to one. Historical use of a track is not legal access.
Landlocked acreage is the classic too-cheap parcel.

**Mineral rights.** Texas routinely severs the mineral estate from the surface
estate, and **the mineral estate is dominant** — its owner can use your surface
to reach the minerals. Ask what mineral interest conveys. Often none does.
Negotiate a surface waiver.

**Restrictive covenants.** With little county zoning, private deed restrictions
do the regulatory work — and they run with the land. They commonly bar
manufactured homes, short-term rentals, subdivision, livestock or business use.
Read the recorded document, not the agent's summary.

## 16. Property tax — exemptions, ag valuation, and the protest

**Homestead exemption.** For a **primary residence** only. As of the 2025 tax
year the school-district homestead exemption is **$140,000** (up from $100,000),
with a further **$60,000** for owners 65+ or with a qualifying disability. It
also brings a **10% annual cap** on assessed value increases. You must **file** —
it is not automatic.

**This is directly actionable for you, and it is one of the better deals in
Texas.** But it attaches to *one* property — your primary residence. If you buy
both a house and a tract of land, the land gets **no exemption and no 10% cap**,
and should be modelled at full market value rising annually. That asymmetry is
large enough to affect which of the two you buy first.

**Agricultural valuation.** An ag-valued tract is taxed on productivity rather
than market value — often an order of magnitude less. Two warnings:

- A listing showing "$400/year taxes" on 10 acres reflects ag valuation, **not**
  what you will pay once you build.
- Changing the use triggers a **rollback**: back taxes plus interest for prior
  years. Confirm the exposure with the appraisal district before buying an ag
  tract to develop.

**Protest your appraisal — annually.** The deadline is **15 May**, or 30 days
after your Notice of Appraised Value, whichever is later, and it is a *received-by*
deadline. In Travis County, informal settlement talks run May–July, formal ARB
hearings June–August. You must file by the deadline to preserve the right to a
formal hearing. In a non-disclosure state the district is guessing at your value;
protesting is normal, and most owners who do it get some reduction.

## 17. What the Austin market actually is right now

Austin is the clearest **buyer's market** among major US metros as of mid-2026,
and understanding that should shape everything about how you bid.

Read those last three together: **more than half of sellers have already cut,
homes sit over two months, and they still close 2.5% under ask.** This is not a
market where you pay list price. Negotiate on price, on the option period, on
closing costs, and on the buyer-agent fee.

It is also a market where **days on market is your best lever.** A property
past 90 days with two cuts has a motivated seller.

## 18. The geography — four corridors

Austin's metro spreads along four axes. Knowing which corridor a listing sits on
tells you most of what you need before opening it.

- **North, I-35** — Round Rock, **Georgetown**, Pflugerville. Established,
  good value, longest-standing suburbs.
- **Northwest, US-183A** — Cedar Park, Leander. Family-oriented, newer stock,
  strong schools.
- **West, Hwy 71 / RR 620** — **Lakeway**, Bee Cave, **Dripping Springs**. The
  Hill Country and Lake Travis. **The priciest corridor**, and where acreage
  lives. Water supply is a genuine constraint here.
- **South, I-35** — **Buda**, **Kyle**, San Marcos, and further out **Bastrop**.
  **The most affordable**, and the fastest-growing.

| Submarket | Months inventory | Character |

| **Wimberley** | ~9.9 | Hill Country, heavily buyer-favouring |

Bee Cave, Lakeway and Dripping Springs run medians above $550,000. Kyle and Buda
sit in the low $300s to mid $400s — the best $/ft² in the metro.

**If your Portuguese search is for land, the Austin analogue is the western and
southern edge**: Dripping Springs, Wimberley, Bastrop. That is where acreage,
buyer-favouring inventory and Hill Country character coincide — and where the
water and septic questions in §15 bite hardest.

## 19. City limits, ETJ, or county — this determines what you can build

Three regimes, and a listing rarely tells you which applies.

**Inside Austin city limits.** Full city zoning, impervious cover limits,
setbacks, watershed regulations, and a **heritage tree ordinance** protecting
large-diameter trees — removal or building near one is a permit matter that can
reshape a site plan. Austin has been progressively loosening single-family lot
rules (smaller minimum lot sizes, more units per lot); if your plan involves
adding a unit or subdividing, **verify the current rule for that specific parcel
with the city**, not from an article. This area has changed repeatedly.

**In Austin's ETJ (extraterritorial jurisdiction).** The city has no zoning power
but controls subdivision and some development standards.

> **This is genuinely unsettled and you must not take an agent's word for it.**
> **SB 2038** (effective September 2023) created a petition process for owners to
> exit a city's ETJ. Austin notified roughly **170 Travis County properties**
> that they were released, then **reversed those releases in March 2026**, citing
> a statutory exception. A nonprofit formed by affected owners **sued the city in
> June 2026**; the matter is live.
>
> If your plan depends on being outside the ETJ, treat that as an **open legal
> question**, get the parcel's status in writing from the City of Austin, and
> re-check it at contract.

**Outside both.** County authority only — subdivision, floodplain, OSSF, road
access. Travis County does not regulate land *use*. The binding limits are then
private: deed restrictions and HOA rules (§15).

## 20. Flooding — check two maps, not one

Austin's floodplain maps are being redrawn upward following the **NOAA Atlas 14**
rainfall study, which found the region at materially higher risk. Current maps
model ~10.2 inches of rain in 24 hours; updated maps reflect nearly **13 inches**
in places, putting thousands of additional structures in higher-risk zones.

**Austin's maps are stricter than FEMA's.** A property can sit outside the FEMA
flood zone and inside Austin's. **Check both.** Austin publishes a lookup at
ATXFloodPro.com alongside the FEMA Map Service Center.

The **Onion Creek corridor** (ZIPs **78748** and **78739**) is the metro's
worst-known flood risk — the October 2013 flood was fatal and prompted a
voluntary buyout that acquired and demolished **over 800 properties**. Homes
remaining outside that buyout carry elevated risk. Treat anything in those ZIPs
as flood-suspect until independently verified, and get a bindable flood
insurance quote before waiving your option period.

Flood status drives insurance, insurance drives financing, and financing drives
resale value. It is not a lifestyle question.

## 21. MUDs — the tax line nobody mentions

Travis County contains roughly **127 taxing entities**, including about **54
MUDs** (Municipal Utility Districts) and 16 emergency services districts.

A MUD funds water, sewer and drainage infrastructure in newer subdivisions
outside city limits, and adds **$0.25–$1.00+ per $100** of value on top of
everything else. On a $500,000 home a $0.75 MUD is roughly **$3,750 per year**,
for decades.

The combined rate inside Austin city limits in Travis County is approximately
**$2.05 per $100** (~2.046%) for 2025–26, of which the City of Austin portion is
about $0.574. Add a MUD and you approach 2.8%.

**Consequence:** a cheaper home in an outlying MUD district can cost more to own
than a dearer one inside the city. Always pull the parcel on **TCAD**, list every
taxing entity, and sum the rates before you tour. Sellers must provide a
statutory **MUD notice** before contract — read it; it discloses the district's
rate and outstanding debt.

## 22. Water in the Hill Country — the constraint that disqualifies parcels

West and south-west of Austin, water availability decides whether land is
buildable. Travis County regulates groundwater availability, and for the
**Trinity Aquifer** the rules are hard:

- **Individual wells require lots of at least 5 acres.**
- A centralised groundwater system allows **3-acre** lots.
- Or the lot count cannot exceed **acreage ÷ 4**.

**A 3-acre tract you intended to put a private well on may simply not qualify.**
Subdivisions relying on wells must additionally prove supply through aquifer
testing and provide a **Groundwater Availability Certification** from a licensed
P.E. or P.G.

Over the **Edwards Aquifer recharge zone**, add water-quality rules, impervious
cover limits and a required water pollution abatement plan — all of which slow
and constrain development.

Budget **3–8 weeks** for septic site evaluation, design and permitting in Travis,
Hays, Comal or Kendall counties, and longer over the recharge zones.

Note the market signal: **Dripping Springs has known water supply challenges**,
and it is also one of the most buyer-favouring submarkets. Those two facts are
related. Cheap Hill Country acreage is frequently cheap for a reason that a site
visit will not reveal but a hydrogeologist will.

## 24. A first-week plan for Austin

1. **Pick two submarkets**, not six. If land: Dripping Springs and Wimberley, or
   Bastrop. If a house: Kyle/Buda for value, Georgetown for establishment.
2. **Build saved searches** on Zillow, Redfin, Realtor.com and — for land —
   LandWatch and LandSearch. Email alerts on, one alert per submarket.
3. **Pull ten parcels on TCAD** and compute the full combined tax rate for each,
   MUD included. This exercise alone will change your budget.
4. **Check every one against both flood maps.**
5. **Record all of them**, including rejects. You need roughly 60–80 before your
   instinct is worth anything.
6. **Find one buyer's agent** who will send sold comps, and negotiate their fee
   in writing before touring — remembering you can ask the seller to cover it.
7. **Do not offer for at least 30 days.** In a market with six months of
   inventory and 55% of listings already cut, patience is the cheapest leverage
   you will ever have.

## Appendix B — the essentials, condensed

1. Choose a metro and two submarkets. Never search "the USA."
2. Use all four national portals; they disagree, and that is useful.
3. For land, the Land.com network and LandSearch are not optional.
4. Learn whether your state discloses sold prices. Texas does not.
5. Get one agent who will send you sold comps.
6. Negotiate your buyer-agent fee in writing, before touring. Ask the seller to pay it.
7. Compute price **plus ten years of property tax** before comparing anything.
8. Pull every parcel on the county appraisal district. Every taxing entity. Every time.
9. Check two flood maps, not one.
10. For land: water, septic, legal access, minerals. In that order. Any one kills the deal.
11. Walk the boundary with a GPS parcel app.
12. Never waive the Texas option period.
13. Read Schedule B of the title commitment.
14. Get a bindable insurance quote, not an estimate.
15. File your homestead exemption. Protest your appraisal every year by 15 May.
16. Buying property gives you no US immigration status. Take tax advice on structure and estate exposure before you buy, not after.
17. Record every listing, including rejections. Sixty to eighty before you trust your instinct.
