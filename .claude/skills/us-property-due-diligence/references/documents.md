# Documents and disclosures — Texas

Portugal's checklist is about proving the property legally exists as described.
Texas's is about finding what limits it, what it costs to hold, and what the
seller already knows and must tell you.

Most of these arrive during the **option period**, which is short. Order them
on day one.

## Before you offer — free, public, do it yourself

| Source | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| **TCAD** (Travis Central Appraisal District; HaysCAD for Hays) | Parcel ID, legal description, acreage, improvement square footage, **assessed** value, exemptions in force, whether an **ag valuation** applies, and the full list of taxing entities on the parcel — this is where a MUD shows up |
| **Travis County Clerk** records | Recorded deeds, plats, easements, restrictive covenants, liens |
| **City of Austin property profile / zoning map** | Zoning, whether the parcel is in city limits or ETJ, impervious cover limits, watershed regulations |
| **Austin floodplain map + FEMA map** | Flood status under both the stricter Austin/Atlas 14 mapping and the federal maps |
| **Travis County OSSF records** | Whether a septic permit exists, and its as-built |

Note what TCAD does **not** give you: the price anything sold for. Assessed
value is not market value and is not a sale price.

## Once under contract

**Title commitment** — issued by the title company, usually seller-paid in
Central Texas. Read all of it, especially **Schedule B** (exceptions: what the
policy does *not* cover — easements, restrictions, mineral reservations) and
**Schedule C** (defects that must be cured before closing). Schedule B is where
the surprises live. Object in writing inside the contract's objection window,
or you accept them.

**Survey + T-47 affidavit** — the T-47 is the seller's sworn statement that an
existing survey remains accurate. If the seller will not sign one, or the survey
is old, buy a new one. The survey is what reveals encroachments, setback
violations, easements crossing the building envelope, and whether the fence is
on the line. On land, this is not optional.

**Seller's Disclosure Notice** — statutorily required for most residential
resales (notable exemptions include some estate, trustee and foreclosure sales,
and new construction). Read it for what is disclosed *and* for the pattern of
"unknown" answers on things an owner would know.

**Statutory notices, where they apply** — MUD notice, PID notice, annexation
notice, water district and, near the coast or in specific districts, others.
The **MUD notice** is the one that matters most around Austin: it discloses the
district's tax rate and outstanding debt. Read it before waiving the option.

**Tax certificate** — the actual current tax bill and any delinquency, from the
tax office rather than an estimate.

**Restrictive covenants / HOA resale certificate** — the covenants themselves,
plus dues, transfer fees, reserves, special assessments and litigation.

**Inspection** — a licensed inspector (TREC-licensed) on a house; a site
evaluation and survey on land. Add specialists where warranted: structural
engineer for foundation movement (Central Texas clay soils make this common),
roof, HVAC, well flow and potability, septic.

**Insurance quote — actual, bindable.** Not an estimate. Hail and wind exposure
is real here, and flood coverage is separate and expensive if any part of the
parcel is in a flood zone. A prior claims history (CLUE report) can make a
property difficult to insure at all.

## At closing

Texas closes through a **title company** rather than a notary. There is **no
state or county real estate transfer tax**, which keeps closing costs lower
than most states. Budget instead for the owner's title policy, survey, lender
fees if financing, and prepaid property tax and insurance escrows.

Buy the **owner's title policy**. Consider the survey deletion/amendment
endorsement, which narrows the standard survey exception.

## After closing

**File for the homestead exemption** if it is your primary residence — it is
not automatic, it is worth a substantial reduction on school-district tax, and
it brings the 10% annual cap on assessed value increases.

**Protest the appraisal.** Annually, and in the first year especially. Because
Texas is non-disclosure, the appraisal district is estimating your value without
knowing what you paid — which cuts both ways and is routinely worth contesting.
The deadline is firm (typically mid-May); missing it costs you the year.
